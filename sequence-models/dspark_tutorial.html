DSpark: Semi-Autoregressive Drafting + Confidence-Scheduled Verification

DeepSeek's production speculative-decoding stack, explained through the one equation it is built to optimize — L = (T_draft + T_verify) / τ — and the one constraint it refuses to break: the output distribution must stay exactly the target model's.

Source: Xin Cheng, Xingkai Yu, Chenze Shao, Jiashi Li, Yunfan Xiong, et al. (Peking University & DeepSeek-AI). "DSpark: Confidence-Scheduled Speculative Decoding with Semi-Autoregressive Generation." arXiv:2607.05147 (July 2026). DeepSeek open-sourced the DSpark checkpoints and DeepSpec, a training repo that also reimplements Eagle3 and DFlash. All facts and numbers below come from the paper. Companion reading in this repo: speculative decoding from scratch and how DFlash trains its draft model — DSpark literally uses a (modified) DFlash as its backbone.

1. Why another drafter? The three levers of speculative decoding

Quick recap. A draft model M_d proposes γ tokens; the target M_t verifies them all in one forward pass by rejection sampling, accepting the longest consistent prefix plus one bonus token. Average latency per generated token: 

L = (T_draft + T_verify) / τ        # τ = expected accepted tokens per cycle (incl. the bonus token)

There are exactly three ways to improve this, and the two drafter families each nail one:

LeverAutoregressive drafters (Eagle3, MTP)Parallel drafters (Medusa, DFlash)
Draft faster (↓ T_draft)✗ — T_draft ∝ γ, forced shallow✓ — one pass, ~constant in γ
Draft better (↑ τ)✓ — each token conditions on the last✗ — positions predicted independently, acceptance decays fast
Verify smarter (↓ effective T_verify)✗ neither — both verify a fixed-length block regardless of content or system load
DSpark's claim is that you can have all three at once: a parallel backbone keeps drafting O(1) in block size, a lightweight sequential head restores intra-block dependencies (draft better), and a confidence-scheduled verifier decides, per request and per system-load, how many draft tokens are even worth sending to the target (verify smarter). The third lever is the novel one — it treats verification length as a global scheduling problem across every request in the batch, not a per-request constant.

2. The problem: parallel drafters win at position 1 and lose everywhere after

The paper's most instructive analysis (its Figure 2) measures position-wise conditional acceptance: at draft position k, among only the cycles where positions 1..k−1 were all accepted, how often is position k accepted too? This strips out the compounding penalty of earlier rejections and exposes each architecture's raw predictive quality per position. Two opposite patterns emerge:

  • Parallel (DFlash) starts high, then decays. Because drafting is one pass regardless of depth, DFlash affords 5 layers vs Eagle3's 1 — pure capacity — and wins position 1 decisively (0.88 vs 0.81 on math; 0.72 vs 0.53 on chat). But each later position marginalizes over all possible predecessors instead of conditioning on the sampled ones, so acceptance slides (0.87 → 0.78 on code, 0.72 → 0.63 on chat).
  • Autoregressive (Eagle3) starts low, then climbs. Shallow, so weak at position 1 — but once earlier tokens lock in a semantic path, later tokens get easier, and Eagle3 exploits that conditional certainty (0.53 → 0.74 on chat).
A parallel drafter is a committee where every member writes one word of the sentence simultaneously, without seeing each other's drafts. If the context admits two continuations — "of course" and "no problem" — the position-1 writer may pick "of" while the position-2 writer, hedging over both modes, picks "problem". Each word is individually plausible; the pair "of problem" is nonsense. This is the classic multi-modal collision from non-autoregressive machine translation (Gu et al. 2018), resurfacing inside a speculative decoder.

Position 1 matters disproportionately: verification is a strict prefix match, so a rejection there throws away the whole block. That's why DFlash beats Eagle3 on total accepted length despite decaying — its position-1 edge has the highest leverage. But the decay caps how much a longer block can help. The obvious synthesis: keep the deep parallel backbone for the initial capacity, add just enough autoregression to stop the suffix rot.

3. Semi-autoregressive generation: a heavy parallel body, a featherweight serial head

DSpark's drafter has two stages. The parallel stage is a modified DFlash backbone: one forward pass over the block produces hidden states h_1..h_γ and base logits U_1..U_γ. (The modification: the anchor token itself becomes the first prediction position, so γ inputs — anchor + γ−1 masks — yield γ draft logits instead of needing γ+1 inputs. Slightly less compute, same quality.)

The sequential stage then samples the block left to right, adding a prefix-dependent transition bias B_k on top of the frozen base logits: 

p_k(v | x_0, x_<k) ∝ exp( U_k(v) + B_k(x_0, x_<k, v) )      # x_0 = anchor from the previous cycle
The expensive tensor — U_k, produced by the deep backbone — is computed once, in parallel. Only the cheap correction B_k runs serially. Since T_sequential ≪ T_parallel, draft latency stays essentially flat in γ (measured: +0.2%–1.3% full-round latency going from draft length 4 to 16, at batch 128), while the factorized distribution P(X|x_0) = Π_k p_k(x_k | x_0, x_<k) is a genuine causal chain — each token conditions on the tokens actually sampled before it. And crucially for speculative decoding, every p_k is an exact softmax: the exact per-token probabilities that rejection sampling needs. (Globally-normalized alternatives like CRF heads can't provide these — their partition function ruins per-token exactness.)

Two instantiations of the bias B_k, in increasing order of memory span:

Markov head (the default)

B_k depends only on the immediately preceding token: a first-order transition matrix B(x_{k−1}, ·). A full V×V table would be enormous, so it's low-rank factorized: 

B = W₁ W₂,   W₁ ∈ ℝ^{V×r},  W₂ ∈ ℝ^{r×V},  r = 256
B(x_{k−1}, ·) = W₁[x_{k−1}] · W₂          # one embedding lookup + one r×V matvec per step

Once position 1 samples "of", the row W₁[of]·W₂ boosts "course" and suppresses "problem" at position 2 — the collision dissolves.

RNN head

The Markov head is memoryless beyond one step. The RNN head carries a recurrent state s_k ∈ ℝ^r across the block: each step concatenates z_k = [s_{k−1}; W₁[x_{k−1}]; h_k] and applies a single gated update (s_k = σ(W_g z_k) ⊙ s_{k−1} + (1−σ(W_g z_k)) ⊙ tanh(W_c z_k)), emitting B_k = W₂ᵀ tanh(W_o z_k). Full prefix memory — but the ablation says it only helps marginally, and mainly at long proposal lengths. Given the deployment friction of a recurrent loop, DeepSeek ships the Markov head.

"A little autoregression goes a long way": with block size fixed at 7, a 2-layer DSpark already beats the 5-layer DFlash baseline across math, code, and chat. Adding one serial rank-256 bigram correction buys more accepted length than tripling the parallel stack's depth. And the gap widens with block size: +16/15/18% over DFlash (math/code/chat) at γ=7 grows to +30/26/22% at γ=15, because DSpark decays so much more slowly along the block.

4. The confidence head: predicting a token's survival odds before verification

Long, high-quality drafts solve the modeling problem but create a systems one: is it worth verifying all γ tokens? Acceptance varies wildly by domain (structured code sustains far longer accepted spans than open-ended chat), and the trailing tokens of a chat draft are likely dead weight. DSpark's first ingredient for "verify smarter" is a per-position confidence head — a single linear probe: 

c_k = σ( wᵀ [ h_k ; W₁[x_{k−1}] ] )      # h_k: backbone hidden state, W₁[x_{k−1}]: Markov embedding of the sampled predecessor

c_k estimates the conditional probability that draft token k survives verification given that positions 1..k−1 were all accepted. By the chain rule, the probability that the whole prefix up to j survives is the running product a_j = Π_{i≤j} c_i. Supervision is elegant: the true per-step acceptance rate has a closed form from speculative-sampling theory, 

c*_k = 1 − ½ · ‖ p_d^k − p_t^k ‖₁        # 1 − total variation distance between draft and target distributions

so the head is trained with binary cross-entropy against this analytical soft label — no extra rollouts needed, just the two distributions already computed during training.

Post-hoc calibration: Sequential Temperature Scaling

Threshold-style heuristics only need confidences to rank tokens correctly. DSpark's scheduler needs their absolute magnitudes — it literally computes expected accepted tokens as sums of the a_j. Raw neural confidences are overconfident (ECE 3–8% despite good discrimination, ROC-AUC 0.81–0.90). The fix, Sequential Temperature Scaling: walk positions left to right, and at each position grid-search one temperature scalar that minimizes the calibration error of the cumulative product, holding earlier positions fixed. Temperature scaling is order-preserving — rankings survive — and average ECE drops to ~1%.

A static threshold sweep validates the head in isolation: pruning tokens below a confidence bar raises chat's overall acceptance rate from 45.7% to 95.7% (math: 76.9→92.5%, code: 67.6→92.0%) by discarding exactly the tokens that would have been rejected anyway. Chat prunes hardest — high-entropy domains are where fixed-length verification wastes the most.

5. The hardware-aware prefix scheduler: verification length as a throughput problem

Here is the paper's central systems insight. A static confidence threshold ignores load: under light traffic, verifying a dubious token is nearly free (the GPU had idle capacity anyway); under heavy traffic, that same token occupies batch capacity another request needed. So DSpark frames verification-length selection across all R active requests as one optimization: 

maximize   Θ = τ · SPS(B)
where      B  = Σ_r (1 + ℓ_r)                    # total tokens sent to the target this step
           τ  = Σ_r (1 + Σ_{j≤ℓ_r} a_{r,j})      # expected accepted tokens (the +1s are bonus tokens)
           SPS(B) = engine steps-per-second at batch size B   # profiled once at engine init

The structure makes this easy to solve greedily. Since a_{r,j} is non-increasing in j, the marginal gain from extending request r's verification from j−1 to j is exactly a_{r,j}. So: pool every candidate token from every request, sort globally by survival probability, and admit them one by one — updating Θ = τ·SPS(B) from the profiled cost table after each admission — and stop the moment Θ drops. The sort order automatically respects prefix structure (a token's survival probability is never higher than its predecessor's), and the early stop is load-awareness incarnate: when SPS(B) is flat (idle GPU), nearly everything gets admitted; when SPS(B) is falling steeply (saturated GPU), only near-certain tokens make the cut.

It's an airline seat-upgrade queue. Every draft token is a standby passenger whose "show-up probability" is its survival estimate a_{r,j}. Boarding one more standby is free while the plane has empty seats (flat SPS), but once you'd have to swap to a bigger, slower aircraft (SPS cliff), you only board passengers near-certain to show. Sorting globally by probability and stopping when expected passengers-per-hour drops is precisely the greedy admission loop.

6. Why it stays lossless: the non-anticipating property

Speculative decoding's exactness proof requires that the decision to admit draft token k for verification must not depend on the value of token k (or anything downstream of it). DSpark's confidence head threatens this subtly: c_{k+1} is computed from the Markov embedding of the sampled token x_k. So if the scheduler searched past a throughput dip to check whether later admissions pay off, the continuation-dependent c_{k+1} would leak information about x_k back into x_k's own admission decision.

The paper's Appendix A counterexample, in full. One request, γ=2, first-token survival a₁ = 0.8, capacity curve SPS(1)=1.0, SPS(2)=0.5, SPS(3)=0.45. Then Θ₀ = 1·1.0 = 1.0 and Θ₁ = 1.8·0.5 = 0.9 — a dip. A retrospective scheduler peeks at Θ₂, which depends on c₂, which depends on the sampled x₁: if x₁ leads to c₂=0.9, Θ₂ = 2.52·0.45 = 1.134 → admit x₁ (ℓ=2); if x₁ leads to c₂=0, Θ₂ = 1.8·0.45 = 0.81 → drop x₁ (ℓ=0). The token's own value decided its admission. Concretely, with target (0.7, 0.3) and draft (0.5, 0.5) over {A, B}, this scheduler outputs A with probability 0.85 instead of 0.7 — the target distribution is broken. The early-stopping break in the greedy loop prevents exactly this: it halts at the Θ₁ < Θ₀ dip, before any continuation-dependent quantity is ever consulted.

One catch: stopping at the first dip finds the global optimum only if Θ is unimodal in the admission count — true for a smoothly decaying SPS curve, false for real hardware. The production fix is in §9, and it's clever: make the search global again, but feed it only stale information that cannot depend on the current tokens.

7. Training: three losses, one frozen target

Training mirrors DFlash's recipe (anchors sampled from target sequences to form γ-token blocks; target model frozen; embedding and LM head shared with the target and frozen; only the backbone, sequential head, and confidence head update). Every term is weighted by the same position-decay w_k = exp(−(k−1)/γ) — early block positions carry more expected acceptance length, so they carry more gradient. The objective stacks three losses: 

L = 0.1 · L_ce  +  0.9 · L_tv  +  1.0 · L_conf

L_ce   = − Σ_k w_k · log p_d^k(x*_k)                    # predict the target's actual next token
L_tv   =   Σ_k w_k · ‖ p_d^k − p_t^k ‖₁                 # match the target's full distribution
L_conf = − Σ_k w_k · BCE( c_k , c*_k )                  # confidence head vs analytical acceptance label
Note the weighting: the total-variation loss dominates cross-entropy 9-to-1. That's because TV distance is the acceptance rate — per-step acceptance equals 1 − ½‖p_d − p_t‖₁ exactly — so minimizing L_tv directly maximizes the quantity speculative decoding cares about. Cross-entropy is just a stabilizing auxiliary. And L_conf gets its labels for free from the same two distributions.

9. Production deployment in DeepSeek-V4

DSpark replaced MTP-1 (single-token multi-token-prediction drafting, the DeepSeek-V3-era baseline) as the production drafter for DeepSeek-V4-Flash and V4-Pro two weeks after the V4-preview release. The deployed configuration: a parallel backbone of 3 MoE layers with a 128-token sliding window, block size γ=5, Markov head, confidence head calibrated with STS. Notably, MTP-3/5 (just verifying more static tokens) had always been rejected in production because it strictly degrades throughput under high concurrency — the scheduler is what makes long drafts safe to deploy.

Making the scheduler real

Algorithm 1 assumes a smooth SPS curve and a scheduler that can act synchronously between steps. Real engines have jagged, cliff-shaped SPS(B) (kernel tiling boundaries), and Zero-Overhead Scheduling needs the next step's batch size before the current step finishes. The deployed adaptation: the truncation capacity K for the upcoming step is estimated from confidence outputs two steps prior, while the actual candidate ranking still uses up-to-date survival scores — a dynamic top-K where the K is stale but the ranking is fresh. This buys two things at once. (1) Scheduling latency disappears into the pipeline, keeping CUDA graphs / ZOS intact. (2) The early-stopping break can be removed — the global search across jagged SPS cliffs is now safe, because decisions depend only on two-step-old information, which forms a causal barrier: no current token's value can influence its own admission. Losslessness is preserved not by restricting the search, but by making its inputs stale.

Two further engineering notes. Training-side: instead of shipping full-vocabulary target logits (V≈10⁵) between workers, only pre-LM-head hidden states move (O(d) per token), with the shared frozen head applied locally; and draft blocks are packed via anchor-bounded token-level attention indices, decoupling draft training cost from target context length. Inference-side: variable per-request verification lengths break fixed-query-length decode kernels, so tokens are flattened into one stream with sequence structure carried by a marker tensor — on DeepSeek-V4 only the index-attention and compress kernels needed modification.

The mechanism is visible in the telemetry: under moderate concurrency the scheduler expands per-request verification budgets to ~4–6 tokens (vs MTP-1's static 2), converting idle compute into accepted tokens; as concurrency saturates the target, budgets shrink smoothly, protecting batch capacity. The paper is careful about the huge strict-SLA percentages: they mostly mean the baseline collapses to a tiny feasible batch there, i.e. DSpark unlocks interactivity tiers that previously didn't exist, rather than being 7× faster at a healthy operating point.

Stated limitation: the parallel backbone always pays the full γ-block drafting cost up front, even for hard requests where little will be accepted — difficulty-aware early exit inside the drafter is left as future work.
