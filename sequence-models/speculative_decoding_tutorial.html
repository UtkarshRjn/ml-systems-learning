Why speculative decoding exists

A large autoregressive model like Llama-70B generates one token at a time. Each token needs a full forward pass over billions of parameters. In practice, on modern GPUs, that forward pass is memory-bandwidth bound, not compute bound: you spend most wall-clock time streaming the weights from HBM into on-chip SRAM, and almost none of it actually multiplying.

A forward pass that produces 1 token uses roughly the same wall-clock time as a forward pass that produces k tokens, as long as k is small (say ≤ 8). The bottleneck is loading weights, not the arithmetic on the activations.

This is the whole opening for speculative decoding. If a cheap draft model can propose a plausible next few tokens, and the expensive target model can verify all of them in a single forward pass, we spend one target step to move the sequence forward by (hopefully) many tokens.

The two contracts

ComponentWhat it producesCost
Draft model $M_q$ A conditional distribution $q(x \mid \text{context})$ over next tokens, cheap to sample from. Small — one draft forward per proposed token.
Target model $M_p$ The conditional distribution $p(x \mid \text{context})$ we actually want to sample from. Large — but one forward evaluates $p$ at every drafted position in parallel.
Think of the draft model as a legal assistant preparing a contract. They write $\gamma$ clauses in advance. The partner (target) reads the whole draft in one sitting and signs off clause-by-clause until they hit one they'd have written differently. From that point onward, the partner writes themselves.

The two things we want to prove

  1. Correctness. The tokens we emit must be distributed exactly as if we had sampled them from $p$ directly. No approximation, no bias toward the draft.
  2. Speedup. The expected number of tokens produced per target forward pass must be $> 1$, and the wall-clock cost (target + $\gamma$ drafts) must be low enough that the trade is favorable.

The next tab derives both, from the rejection sampling identity up.

Basics — intuition before algorithm

Before we look at the algorithm and the correctness proof, two ideas are worth building up intuition for on their own:

  1. Where does the accept rule $\min(1, p(x)/q(x))$ come from? Why that specific formula, and why the cap at 1?
  2. What does the phrase "averaged over the sampling distribution" mean when we define the acceptance rate $\alpha$? Which distribution are we averaging over, and why does it matter?

Both feel mysterious until you look at them from the right angle; after that they're inevitable.

Where do the accept probabilities come from?

The algorithm we'll see in the next tab accepts a drafted token $x$ with probability $\min(1, p(x)/q(x))$. Let's understand intuitively why this specific formula, and why the cap at 1.

Every emitted token $x$ arrives via exactly one of two paths:

  • Path 1 — draft path: the draft picked $x$ and we accepted it.
  • Path 2 — residual path: the draft picked something else, we rejected it, and we resampled from $p'$.

For the output frequency of $x$ to equal $p(x)$, the two paths must add up:

$$\underbrace{q(x) \cdot a(x)}_{\text{path 1}} \;+\; \underbrace{P(\text{reject}) \cdot p'(x)}_{\text{path 2}} \;=\; p(x).$$

Unpacking the equation, symbol by symbol

  • $q(x)$ — probability the draft samples $x$ in the first place (the draft samples from its own distribution $q$).
  • $a(x)$ — probability we accept $x$, given that $x$ was drafted.
  • $q(x) \cdot a(x)$ — joint probability that "the draft sampled $x$ AND we accepted it." This is path 1's contribution to the frequency of $x$ emissions.
  • $P(\text{reject})$ — overall probability we ended up rejecting the draft (summed over all tokens the draft might have picked). Call this $Z$.
  • $p'(x)$ — probability of drawing $x$ when we resample from the residual. Importantly, $p'$ doesn't depend on which token got rejected — once the algorithm rejects, the drafted token is thrown out and we sample fresh from $p'$.
  • $P(\text{reject}) \cdot p'(x)$ — joint probability that "we rejected AND the resample happened to be $x$." This is path 2's contribution.

There's no third way to emit a token — the accept/reject decision is strict either/or. So $P(\text{output} = x) = $ path 1 $+$ path 2, and correctness requires this to equal $p(x)$.

Quick numerical sanity check

Using the tutorial's toy distributions with $\alpha = 0.85$ (so $Z = 0.15$, and the residual $p' = (0.667, 0, 0, 0, 0.333)$ on tokens A–E):

Token B (over-supplied: $p(B) = 0.30$, $q(B) = 0.40$, $a(B) = 0.75$, $p'(B) = 0$):

$$q(B) \cdot a(B) + Z \cdot p'(B) = 0.40 \cdot 0.75 + 0.15 \cdot 0 = 0.30 = p(B). \checkmark$$

All B-emissions come from path 1; path 2 never produces B.

Token A (under-supplied: $p(A) = 0.20$, $q(A) = 0.10$, $a(A) = 1.00$, $p'(A) = 0.667$):

$$q(A) \cdot a(A) + Z \cdot p'(A) = 0.10 \cdot 1.00 + 0.15 \cdot 0.667 = 0.10 + 0.10 = 0.20 = p(A). \checkmark$$

Half of A's emissions come from path 1, half from path 2.

Applying this bookkeeping to each token separately pins down $a(x)$ completely.

Case 1: $p(x) < q(x)$ — draft over-proposes this token

The residual $p'(x) = \max(0, p(x) - q(x))/Z$ has zero mass here, so path 2 contributes nothing. The only way to emit $x$ is path 1, and it must deliver frequency $p(x)$ on its own:

$$q(x) \cdot a(x) = p(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} < 1.$$

This is a genuine "reject some of the time" probability. Its exact value $p(x)/q(x)$ is forced — anything smaller under-emits $x$, anything larger over-emits it.

Case 2: $p(x) \ge q(x)$ — draft under-proposes this token

Even if we accept every single draft of $x$ ($a(x) = 1$), path 1 only delivers frequency $q(x) < p(x)$. There's a shortage of $p(x) - q(x)$ that must come from somewhere.

That "somewhere" is exactly the residual. When the draft picks some other token $y$ (from an over-represented bucket) and we reject it, we resample from $p'$ — which puts positive mass precisely on the under-represented tokens like $x$. Path 2 contributes:

$$P(\text{reject}) \cdot p'(x) = Z \cdot \frac{p(x) - q(x)}{Z} = p(x) - q(x).$$

Adding both paths: $q(x) + (p(x) - q(x)) = p(x)$. ✓

Why is $a(x) = 1$ forced (rather than some smaller value)? Because we can't accept with probability greater than 1. If we could, we'd set $a(x) = p(x)/q(x) > 1$ and skip the residual entirely — but probabilities cap at 1, so we accept everything and let the residual make up the shortfall. That cap is exactly what the $\min(1, \cdot)$ in the formula encodes.

You want a cart of groceries matching recipe $p$. Your friend (draft) shops using their preferences $q$.
SituationWhat you doWhy
Friend brings too many onions ($q > p$)Keep only fraction $p/q$, toss the rest backOtherwise you'd have too many onions relative to the recipe
Friend brings too few garlic cloves ($q < p$)Keep every single oneEvery clove counts — you're behind on garlic
End of trip: cart is short on garlicGo back yourself and grab garlic (residual)The friend won't fix it; you top up from the pantry

The min(1, p/q) rule is: "keep everything if you're short, thin proportionally if you're over." The residual handles the topping-up.

Common confusion: "If the draft over-proposes token B and I reject some, shouldn't I also emit B from the residual sometimes?" No — that's the elegance. When you reject B, you resample from $p'$, which puts zero mass on B (because B is over-represented, not under-represented). Rejected B's are always swapped for tokens the draft was under-representing. Every rejection is a redistribution.

Where does the residual formula $p'(x) = \max(0, p(x) - q(x))/Z$ come from?

We just used the residual to derive the accept probabilities. Now let's go the other way: given the accept rule, what does the residual formula have to look like? The same two-path equation forces it.

Intuition: deficit accounting

For each token, compare draft supply $q(x)$ to target demand $p(x)$:

  • Over-supplied tokens ($q > p$): the draft brings too many. The accept rule throttles them down via $a(x) = p(x)/q(x)$. Path 1 delivers exactly $p(x)$. No help needed from the residual — it should give these tokens zero mass.
  • Under-supplied tokens ($p > q$): even if we accept every draft ($a(x) = 1$), path 1 only delivers $q(x)$ — leaving a deficit of $p(x) - q(x)$ unfilled. This deficit must come from the residual.

So the residual's job is to supply exactly the profile of deficits:

$$\text{residual delivers } \max(0, p(x) - q(x)) \text{ to each token } x.$$

The $\max(0, \cdot)$ zeros out the over-supplied tokens (they have no deficit). That's the numerator.

Why divide by $Z$?

The residual $p'$ has to be a proper probability distribution — its values must sum to 1. But $\max(0, p(x) - q(x))$ doesn't sum to 1; it sums to some smaller number:

$$Z \;=\; \sum_x \max(0, p(x) - q(x)).$$

$Z$ is the total unfilled deficit across all under-supplied tokens. Dividing by $Z$ turns the deficit profile into a valid probability distribution:

$$p'(x) \;=\; \frac{\max(0, p(x) - q(x))}{Z}.$$

The beautiful coincidence: $Z = P(\text{reject})$

Here's why the arithmetic closes exactly. The total surplus from over-supplied tokens equals the total deficit from under-supplied tokens — because $p$ and $q$ both sum to 1:

$$\underbrace{\sum_x \max(0, q(x) - p(x))}_{\text{total surplus}} \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_x \max(0, p(x) - q(x))}_{\text{total deficit}} \;=\; Z.$$

And the total rejection rate is exactly the total surplus (we only reject over-supplied tokens, and we reject them at rate $1 - p(x)/q(x)$, contributing $q(x) - p(x)$ per over-supplied token):

$$P(\text{reject}) \;=\; \sum_x q(x) \cdot (1 - a(x)) \;=\; \sum_x \max(0, q(x) - p(x)) \;=\; Z.$$

So $P(\text{reject}) \cdot p'(x) = Z \cdot \max(0, p(x) - q(x))/Z = \max(0, p(x) - q(x))$ — the residual delivers precisely what's needed at each token, no more, no less. The residual is invoked exactly as often as needed to fill the deficits.

Algebraic derivation from the two-path equation

Same conclusion the mechanical way: plug the accept rule $a(x) = \min(1, p(x)/q(x))$ into the two-path equation and solve for $p'(x)$.

Case 1: $p(x) < q(x)$. Then $a(x) = p(x)/q(x)$, so

$$q(x) \cdot \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} + Z \cdot p'(x) = p(x) \;\Longrightarrow\; p(x) + Z \cdot p'(x) = p(x) \;\Longrightarrow\; p'(x) = 0.$$

Case 2: $p(x) \ge q(x)$. Then $a(x) = 1$, so

$$q(x) + Z \cdot p'(x) = p(x) \;\Longrightarrow\; p'(x) = \frac{p(x) - q(x)}{Z}.$$

Combining: $p'(x) = \max(0, p(x) - q(x)) / Z$. ✓

Notice the residual is concentrated on exactly the under-supplied tokens (A and E), and their probabilities are proportional to how badly under-supplied they were: A had twice the deficit of E, so A gets twice E's residual mass.

The residual is the target's leftover shopping list — the exact profile of tokens the draft failed to supply enough of, renormalized into a probability distribution so we can sample from it. Every time we reject, we go back to this list and grab one item to top up the missing mass.

The acceptance rate $\alpha$

Now let's define the single most important quantity in speculative decoding: the per-position acceptance rate $\alpha$, the probability that a given draft token survives verification.

$$\alpha \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\!\left[\min\!\left(1, \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)\right] = \sum_x \min(p(x), q(x)) = 1 - \text{TV}(p, q).$$

The clean interpretation: $\alpha$ is the overlap between $p$ and $q$, or equivalently one minus their total-variation distance. A draft model that is close to the target in TV distance has high acceptance. This is what the next tab's expected-tokens formula hinges on.

What does "averaged over the sampling distribution" mean?

The subscript $x \sim q$ under the expectation is worth being precise about, because there are two distributions floating around ($p$ and $q$) and mixing them up is a common source of confusion.

For a specific drafted token $x$, the accept probability is $\min(1, p(x)/q(x))$ (from the previous section). Different tokens have different accept probabilities. But we don't get to pick which token becomes the draft — the draft samples it from $q$. So the per-token accept rate we actually observe averages the per-token accept probability weighted by how often that token is the one drafted, which is exactly $q(x)$:

$$\alpha \;=\; \underbrace{\sum_x}_{\text{over tokens}} \underbrace{q(x)}_{\substack{\text{how often}\\ x \text{ is drafted}}} \cdot \underbrace{\min\!\left(1, \tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right)}_{\text{accept prob if drafted}}.$$

Using toy distributions over five tokens (we'll reuse these in the Vanilla SD tab and the playground):

token$q(x)$$p(x)$accept prob if drafted = $\min(1, p/q)$
A0.100.201.000
B0.400.300.750
C0.300.301.000
D0.150.100.667
E0.050.101.000

Token B is drafted 40% of the time and accepts 75% of the time. Token E has accept prob 1.00, but the draft only picks it 5% of the time. B's mediocre accept probability matters much more than E's perfect one — because B is drafted $8\times$ more often.

Weighting each token's accept probability by how often it's drafted:

$$\alpha \;=\; 0.10(1.00) + 0.40(0.75) + 0.30(1.00) + 0.15(0.667) + 0.05(1.00) \;=\; 0.85.$$
If you naively averaged the accept probabilities without weighting by $q$, you'd get $(1.00 + 0.75 + 1.00 + 0.667 + 1.00) / 5 \approx 0.883$ — wrong, because that treats all tokens as equally likely to be drafted, and they aren't. Any time you see "expected value" in probability, there's an implicit "with respect to which distribution?" — and here the answer is $q$, because $q$ is what produces the sample.
Takeaways from this tab, before we move on:
  • The accept rule $\min(1, p/q)$ is the unique per-token acceptance probability that makes the output frequency equal $p(x)$ (given the two-path structure of the algorithm).
  • The cap at 1 is a probability-can't-exceed-1 constraint; the residual $p'$ fills the shortfall.
  • $\alpha = 1 - \text{TV}(p, q)$ is the overlap between $p$ and $q$, weighted by $q$ (the draft's own sampling distribution) — not a naive average across tokens.

Vanilla speculative decoding (linear draft)

This is the algorithm from Leviathan et al. (2023) and Chen et al. (2023). One draft, $\gamma$ tokens deep, one target verification pass.

The algorithm

Given: context c, target p, draft q, draft length γ

Repeat until end-of-sequence:
  1. Draft:  sample x₁, x₂, ..., x₀ sequentially from q, each conditioned
             on c and prior x₂₂.
  2. Verify: run ONE target forward on (c, x₁, ..., x₀) to get
             p(· | c, x₂<i) for i = 1 ... γ+1  (all in parallel).
  3. For i = 1 ... γ:
       r ∼ Uniform(0, 1)
       If r ≤ p(x₂) / q(x₂):        accept x₂
       Else:                          reject, resample x₂' ∼ p′, break
     Where p′(x) = max(0, p(x) - q(x)) / Z   with Z = ∑₃ max(0, p(x) - q(x))
  4. If all γ drafts accepted: sample bonus token x₀₊₁ ∼ p directly.
  5. Emit accepted tokens (+ resampled or bonus token). Extend context.
Step 3 is the whole trick. It is not ordinary rejection sampling. Ordinary rejection sampling with a proposal $q$ and target $p$ needs an envelope constant $M$ with $Mq(x) \ge p(x)$ everywhere, which we don't have. The modified rejection rule used here reuses the draft sample when possible and resamples from the specific residual $p'$ when it doesn't — and this exact construction is what makes the output distribution equal $p$.

Why the residual has that specific form

Fix a position. Let $A$ be the event "we accept the draft" and let $X$ be the token we ultimately emit. We want $P(X = x) = p(x)$ for every $x$.

Compute $P(X = x)$ by conditioning on whether the draft was accepted at this position.

Accept branch. The draft was sampled from $q$ and accepted with probability $\min(1, p(x)/q(x))$: $$P(X = x \wedge A) = q(x) \cdot \min\!\left(1, \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right) = \min(p(x), q(x)).$$

Reject branch. The overall rejection probability (summing over which token was drafted) is $$P(\bar A) = \sum_y q(y)\left(1 - \min\!\left(1, \frac{p(y)}{q(y)}\right)\right) = \sum_y \max(0, q(y) - p(y)).$$ Since $\sum_y p(y) = \sum_y q(y) = 1$, we also have $\sum_y \max(0, q(y) - p(y)) = \sum_y \max(0, p(y) - q(y)) = Z$. So $P(\bar A) = Z$.

Conditional on rejection, we resample $X$ from $p'(x) = \max(0, p(x) - q(x))/Z$, giving $$P(X = x \wedge \bar A) = Z \cdot \frac{\max(0, p(x) - q(x))}{Z} = \max(0, p(x) - q(x)).$$

Add them. $$P(X = x) = \min(p(x), q(x)) + \max(0, p(x) - q(x)).$$ Case 1: $p(x) \ge q(x)$. Then $\min = q(x)$ and $\max = p(x) - q(x)$, sum is $p(x)$. ✓
Case 2: $p(x) < q(x)$. Then $\min = p(x)$ and $\max = 0$, sum is $p(x)$. ✓

Either way $P(X = x) = p(x)$. The output at every position is distributed exactly as if sampled from $p$. $\square$

The residual $p'$ is not the same as $p$ restricted to the tokens where $p > q$. It is $p - q$ restricted to positive values, then renormalized. Concretely, if a token has $p(x) = 0.1$ and $q(x) = 0.3$, then $p'(x) = 0$ — you will never resample that token after a rejection, even though $p(x)$ itself is nonzero. That's fine because the draft has already been over-representing it, and the accept branch already accounts for the $\min(p, q) = 0.1$ worth of probability mass.

Why the $\gamma+1$-th position gets a free "bonus" token

Notice step 2 asks the target for $p(\cdot \mid c, x_{<i})$ for $i = 1, \ldots, \gamma + 1$ — one past the last draft token. That extra distribution is available at zero additional cost because the target forward pass produces logits at every input position anyway.

If all $\gamma$ drafts are accepted, the algorithm samples the $(\gamma+1)$-th token directly from that already-computed $p(\cdot \mid c, x_1, \ldots, x_\gamma)$. This is the "bonus" token. It's why the best case yields $\gamma + 1$ tokens per target step, not $\gamma$.

Expected number of tokens per target step

Recall from the Basics tab that the per-position acceptance rate is $\alpha = 1 - \text{TV}(p, q)$ — the overlap between draft and target distributions.

Assume the per-position acceptance probability is $\alpha$ at every position (the standard simplifying assumption in the analysis). Let $N$ be the number of consecutively accepted drafts (out of $\gamma$). Then $P(N \ge i) = \alpha^i$ for $i \le \gamma$, so

$$\mathbb{E}[N] = \sum_{i=1}^{\gamma} P(N \ge i) = \sum_{i=1}^{\gamma} \alpha^i = \frac{\alpha(1 - \alpha^\gamma)}{1 - \alpha}.$$

Every iteration emits exactly one extra token beyond $N$ — either the resample from $p'$ (if a draft was rejected) or the bonus token (if all $\gamma$ were accepted). So the expected number of tokens per target forward pass is

$$\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per step}] = \mathbb{E}[N] + 1 = \frac{1 - \alpha^{\gamma + 1}}{1 - \alpha}.\;}$$

This is the classic Leviathan formula.

Worked example

Suppose $p$ and $q$ are the following distributions over five tokens:

tokenABCDE
$q(x)$0.100.400.300.150.05
$p(x)$0.200.300.300.100.10
$\min(p,q)$0.100.300.300.100.05
$\max(0, p-q)$0.100.000.000.000.05

Then $\alpha = \sum \min(p, q) = 0.85$ and $Z = \sum \max(0, p - q) = 0.15$ (equal, as they must be). The residual is $p' = (10/15,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 5/15) = (0.667,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0.333)$ concentrated on the tokens the draft was under-representing.

With $\gamma = 4$: expected tokens per target step $= (1 - 0.85^5)/(1 - 0.85) \approx 3.71$. So one expensive target pass yields, on average, nearly four tokens instead of one — a ~$3.7\times$ speedup in target passes. (Multiply by $1/(1 + c\gamma)$ to convert target passes into wall-clock — with $c = 0.05$, wall-clock speedup drops to $3.71/1.2 \approx 3.1\times$.)

The wall-clock speedup

Total wall-clock per iteration is roughly (one target forward) $+$ ($\gamma$ draft forwards). If the draft costs $c$ times a target forward, then

$$\text{speedup} \;\approx\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per step}]}{1 + c\gamma} \;=\; \frac{1 - \alpha^{\gamma + 1}}{(1 - \alpha)(1 + c\gamma)}.$$

This function has an interior optimum in $\gamma$: too small and you underuse the target's parallel-verification capacity; too large and the draft cost $c\gamma$ swamps the gain, because deep drafts get cut off by early rejections anyway. In practice $\gamma \in [4, 8]$ is where most systems land for chat models with a small draft.

Tree drafts — many candidates verified at once

Linear speculative decoding wastes work on rejection: the moment draft token $x_i$ is rejected, all downstream draft tokens $x_{i+1}, \ldots, x_\gamma$ get thrown away, even though the target already spent compute on them. Tree drafts fix this by proposing multiple candidates at each position, so a single rejection along one branch doesn't kill all future work.

A tree draft turns the draft's per-position uncertainty into extra candidates. Instead of one path of length $\gamma$ (linear), the draft proposes a tree of $T$ nodes covering many depth-$\gamma$ paths. The target still runs one forward pass — over all $T$ nodes — thanks to a specially crafted attention mask.

Tree attention: verifying $T$ nodes in one pass

A draft tree has a root at the current end-of-context token; each edge is a candidate next token. A node at depth $d$ represents a particular length-$d$ continuation.

To evaluate the target on all tree nodes at once, we flatten the tree into a token sequence and give the target a node-specific attention mask: node $v$ can attend to (a) the original context, and (b) exactly the ancestors of $v$ in the tree. Sibling branches are invisible to each other. 

Example tree (root R, two children a,b; a has children c,d):

  R --a-- c
       \-- d
   \-b

Flatten to sequence: [context] R a b c d
Attention mask (rows = query, cols = key; 1 = visible):

        ctx   R   a   b   c   d
  ctx    1    .   .   .   .   .
  R      1    1   .   .   .   .
  a      1    1   1   .   .   .
  b      1    1   .   1   .   .
  c      1    1   1   .   1   .
  d      1    1   1   .   .   1

With this mask, one forward gives the target's $p(\cdot \mid \text{context}, \text{path to } v)$ at every tree node $v$ — everything we need to verify every candidate path.

Multi-round rejection sampling (SpecInfer / SpecTr)

Now suppose at a single position we have $k$ iid candidates $x^{(1)}, \ldots, x^{(k)}$ from the draft $q$, and we want to output a token distributed exactly as $p$. Miao et al. (SpecInfer) and Sun et al. (SpecTr) generalize modified rejection sampling to $k$ candidates:

Input: candidates $x^{(1)}, \ldots, x^{(k)} \sim q$ iid; target $p$.

Initialize: $p_0 \leftarrow p$.

For $j = 1, \ldots, k$:

  • Accept $x^{(j)}$ with probability $\min\!\left(1,\ p_{j-1}(x^{(j)}) / q(x^{(j)})\right)$.
  • If accepted: return $x^{(j)}$.
  • If rejected: $p_j(x) \leftarrow \max(0,\ p_{j-1}(x) - q(x)) / Z_j$, where $Z_j = \sum_x \max(0,\ p_{j-1}(x) - q(x))$.

If all $k$ rejected: return a fresh sample from $p_k$.

Every round uses the same draft distribution $q$, but tries to accept against a shrinking target $p_j$ that reflects the probability mass still unexplained by previous rejections.

Induction on $j$: assuming the algorithm run starting from target $p_{j-1}$ produces samples exactly from $p_{j-1}$, we show the same is true starting from $p_j = \text{norm}(\max(0, p_{j-1}-q))$ after one round of processing candidate $x^{(j)}$.

Fix $x$. Applying the single-round correctness proof from tab 2 with target $p_{j-1}$ and draft $q$: $$P(X = x \mid \text{start from } p_{j-1}) = \min(p_{j-1}(x), q(x)) + \max(0, p_{j-1}(x) - q(x)) = p_{j-1}(x).$$ So each level of the recursion is unbiased with respect to the current target $p_{j-1}$; the recursion is unrolled from $j = 1$ (target $= p$) upward, so the marginal output distribution is $p$. The final "if all rejected, sample from $p_k$" step is correct because $p_k$ is what $p$ has become after $k$ residualizations, and that mass is exactly the probability of reaching the "all rejected" branch. $\square$

How much does a second candidate buy you?

Diminishing returns. Let $\beta_j = 1 - \text{TV}(p_{j-1}, q)$ be the acceptance rate at round $j$. Then

$$P(\text{some candidate accepted}) = 1 - \prod_{j=1}^{k}(1 - \beta_j).$$

In the toy example from tab 2, $\beta_1 = \alpha = 0.85$. After a round-1 rejection, the target has shifted to $p_1 = p' = (0.667, 0, 0, 0, 0.333)$; the draft's overlap with $p_1$ is $\min(0.667, 0.10) + \min(0.333, 0.05) = 0.15$, so $\beta_2 = 0.15$. The probability that one of the first two candidates is accepted is $1 - (1 - 0.85)(1 - 0.15) = 1 - 0.1275 = 0.8725$. Only a 2.25-percentage-point improvement from adding the second candidate. And $\beta_3$ collapses further.

The draft $q$ over-represents some tokens and under-represents others. The first candidate uses up all the "easy overlap." The second candidate is trying to hit a residual $p'$ that is maximally different from $q$ (that's what "residual" means), so drawing another sample from $q$ has a low chance of falling in that residual's support. Diminishing returns is structural, not incidental.

Putting the tree together

A depth-$\gamma$ tree with branching factor $k$ works like this at verification time (walking depth-first from the root):

  1. At the root's position, apply multi-round rejection sampling against the $k$ children of the root, using the target's distribution at that position. If a child $v^\star$ is accepted, emit it and recurse into the subtree rooted at $v^\star$. If none is accepted, resample from the final residual $p_k$ and stop.
  2. At each accepted node, apply the same procedure to its children. Continue until a rejection or until reaching a leaf. The leaf's target distribution gives you the "bonus" position.

So a tree draft can advance the sequence by up to $\gamma + 1$ tokens per iteration, same as the linear case, but with a higher per-level acceptance probability because you got multiple bites at each level. The cost is that the target now has to verify $T \gg \gamma$ nodes. Whether that is worth it depends on how memory-bound the target still is at sequence length $T$.

Tree drafts are only free-ish while the target's forward pass is memory-bound. Once $T$ is large enough that the target's attention and MLP become compute-bound, extra tree nodes cost linear extra time. In practice trees stay small: ~20–60 nodes for a 7B target, and dynamic (EAGLE-2) rather than static.

EAGLE — drafting in feature space, not token space

Vanilla speculative decoding uses a small language model as the draft. EAGLE (Li et al. 2024) replaces that with a much lighter autoregressive head that predicts the target model's hidden states directly. It is faster, more accurate, and it composes cleanly with tree verification.

Where does draft uncertainty come from?

Suppose the true next-token distribution is close to uniform over two tokens: "I" and "The". A small token-level draft model picks one, say "I". Now it has to draft the token after "I". But the small model doesn't know whether the target would have picked "I" or "The" — and the token that follows "I" vs "The" is very different. That branching uncertainty accumulates fast, and the draft's acceptance rate falls off with depth.

Sampling in token space collapses ambiguity too early: once you commit to "I", you condition on that choice. Sampling in feature space preserves ambiguity — the feature vector after "I or The" can encode a mixture, and the final token-space distribution ($\text{LM head}(f)$) recovers the bimodality. EAGLE exploits this by pushing the draft to feature level and only converting to tokens at the last moment.

The EAGLE draft head

Let $f_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the target model's second-to-last layer hidden state at position $t$, and let $e_t$ be the token embedding at position $t$. EAGLE's draft is a single transformer decoder layer (call it $\Phi$) that takes the concatenation $(f_{t-1}, e_t)$ and predicts $f_t$:

$$\hat f_t = \Phi\big(f_{t-1},\, e_t\big).$$

The draft's next-token distribution is then obtained by running the target's own frozen LM head on $\hat f_t$:

$$q_t(x) = \text{softmax}\big(W_{\text{LM}}\, \hat f_t\big)(x).$$

This is autoregressive on features, not tokens. The draft chain looks like: 

t = 0:  start from target's real f₀ (from the last accepted iteration)
t = 1:  &hat;f₁ = Φ(f₀, e₁)         where e₁ = embedding of the last emitted token
        sample x₁ from q₁ = softmax(W · &hat;f₁)
t = 2:  &hat;f₂ = Φ(&hat;f₁, embed(x₁))
        sample x₂ from q₂ = softmax(W · &hat;f₂)
...

Why this beats a small token-level LM as a draft

Small LM draftEAGLE feature-level draft
Parameters ~10× smaller model, full transformer stack One transformer decoder layer on top of the target's own features
Latency per draft token One forward pass of the small LM One layer + one LM-head projection — much cheaper
Uncertainty handling Commits to a token, loses ambiguity Feature vector encodes the mixture; LM head recovers it
Training Full LM pre-training Feature-regression loss ($\|\hat f_t - f_t\|_2^2$) plus cross-entropy on tokens; the target model is frozen

Because $\Phi$ is trained to mimic the target's own feature trajectory, the draft's $q$ is much closer to the target's $p$ than a general-purpose small LM would be. In TV-distance terms, $\alpha = 1 - \text{TV}(p, q)$ is substantially higher — accepted runs are longer.

The correctness story is unchanged

Even though the draft lives in feature space, at the moment of verification we still have a proper token distribution $q(x)$ from the draft and $p(x)$ from the target. The modified rejection rule from tab 2 applies verbatim. EAGLE changes how you obtain $q$ cheaply and how close it is to $p$, not the correctness contract.

EAGLE-2 — dynamic trees

EAGLE-1 uses a fixed tree shape: at each depth, expand a fixed set of top-$k$ children. But not every branch is equally worth expanding. A high-confidence draft position (softmax nearly deterministic) gets little marginal value from a wide fan-out; a low-confidence position benefits from many children. EAGLE-2 turns this into an online, context-dependent tree.

The confidence signal

At every node $v$ in the growing tree, EAGLE-2 computes an estimate of the cumulative acceptance probability along the path from root to $v$:

$$\hat P(v) = \prod_{u \in \text{path}(v)} q_u(\text{token}_u)$$

where $q_u$ is the draft's softmax at node $u$'s parent. The product of draft-side softmax probabilities is a well-calibrated proxy for target-side acceptance because EAGLE's draft has been trained to mimic the target, so $q \approx p$ and thus $\min(1, p/q) \approx 1$ along high-$q$ paths.

Two-phase tree construction

EAGLE-2 grows the tree with a best-first expansion, then prunes to keep verification cheap.

  1. Expand: maintain a priority queue over frontier nodes keyed by $\hat P(v)$. Repeatedly pop the highest-scoring frontier node, expand it into its top-$k$ children (from the draft's softmax), and push those children back onto the queue. Stop when the tree has grown to a budget of $N_{\text{total}}$ nodes.
  2. Rerank: after expansion, sort all nodes by $\hat P(v)$ and keep only the top $N_{\text{final}}$. This is the final draft tree that gets verified by the target.

So expansion prioritizes exploring where the draft is confident (since deep confident paths have high expected acceptance), while reranking makes sure the fixed-budget verification tree is dominated by the highest-probability continuations across the whole tree, not the fringes of low-confidence branches.

EAGLE-2's tree is beam-search-shaped: put more depth where the draft is sure, more breadth where it isn't, but always cap total compute at $N_{\text{final}}$. It uses the draft's own confidence as the heuristic — which is honest, because we know the draft has been trained to look like the target.

Verification cost in the dynamic-tree regime

The tree attention mask from tab 3 handles any tree shape, so EAGLE-2's per-iteration target cost is:

  • Draft: $O(N_{\text{total}} \cdot d_{\text{layer}})$ — cheap because $\Phi$ is one layer.
  • Target: one forward pass over $N_{\text{final}}$ nodes, with tree attention. Dominated by loading the target's weights (still memory-bound at these sequence lengths).

Empirically, EAGLE-2 lands ~3–5× wall-clock speedup on Llama-class targets with a draft that's ~1% of the target's parameters, compared to ~2× for a well-tuned linear speculative decoder.
