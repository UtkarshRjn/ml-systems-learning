Loss Functions in Machine Learning, in Plain English

A guided tour of every loss you'll actually run into — MSE to Huber, entropy to KL divergence, hinge to focal loss, triplet to InfoNCE, GANs to VAEs. Interactive demos throughout so you can see the numbers move, not just read the formulas.

1. What is a loss function, really?

A loss function takes what your model predicted and what was actually true, and squeezes the difference into a single number: "how wrong were you, and how badly?" Training a model is just turning the crank on gradient descent to make that number smaller.

A loss function is a coach with a whistle. Every prediction gets scored. A gentle miss gets a gentle correction (small gradient). A wild miss gets a loud whistle (large gradient) — but how loud depends entirely on which coach you hired. Some coaches (MSE) yell proportionally to the square of your mistake. Some (MAE) yell the same amount no matter how bad the miss was. Some (hinge) don't even blow the whistle once you've cleared the bar by enough margin. Picking a loss function is picking which coach you want.
Almost every loss in this page is one of two things: a distance between a number and a target (regression), or a divergence between a predicted probability distribution and a true one (classification / generative modeling). Once you see that split, the whole zoo gets a lot smaller.

2. Regression losses: MSE, MAE, Huber

These all answer "how far is my prediction ŷ from the true value y?" — they just disagree on how to punish big misses vs. small ones. Let e = y − ŷ be the error.

LossFormula (per example)Gradient w.r.t. ŷPersonality
MSE (L2)e²−2e (grows with error)Punishes big errors hard. Sensitive to outliers.
MAE (L1)|e|−sign(e) (constant size)Treats every miss the same size-wise. Robust to outliers, but the gradient never shrinks near zero.
Huber0.5·e² if |e|≤δ, else δ·(|e|−0.5δ)Linear in e near 0, then flattens to ±δBest of both: quadratic (smooth) near zero, linear (robust) far away.
Imagine grading essay scores off by a few points vs. off by 50 points (a data-entry typo). MSE squares the gap, so that one typo contributes as much "error signal" as dozens of normal small misses — the model will twist itself to fix that one outlier. MAE shrugs: a 50-point miss and a 5-point miss just get proportionally different, not exponentially different, weight. Huber says: "be precise for small errors (quadratic), but stop overreacting past a threshold δ (linear)."

3. Entropy: the cost of surprise

Before cross-entropy and KL divergence make sense, you need entropy. Entropy measures how "surprising" a distribution is on average, measured in bits (if we use log₂). 

H(P) = − Σ p(x) · log₂ p(x)
If a distribution always predicts the same outcome (e.g. "it's sunny 100% of the time"), there's zero surprise, so entropy is 0 — you already know the answer. If a distribution is a coin flip (50/50), you're maximally unsure, so entropy is at its peak (1 bit). Entropy is the average number of yes/no questions you'd need to ask to pin down the outcome.
Entropy is a property of one distribution — it doesn't compare anything yet. It's the baseline "how hard is this distribution to predict, even for someone who knows it perfectly." Cross-entropy (next section) is what happens when your guess about the distribution is wrong.

4. Cross-entropy: the cost of a wrong guess

Cross-entropy H(P, Q) asks: if the true distribution is P but you believe it's Q, how many bits does it cost you on average to encode outcomes drawn from P, using a code built for Q? 

H(P, Q) = − Σ p(x) · log₂ q(x)

In supervised learning, P is the one-hot true label and Q is your model's predicted probability. For binary classification with true label y ∈ {0,1} and predicted probability ŷ = p(y=1), this collapses to the familiar: 

BCE(y, ŷ) = − [ y · log(ŷ) + (1 − y) · log(1 − ŷ) ]
Notice the shape: when y=1, only the log(ŷ) term survives. As ŷ → 0 (you were confidently wrong), −log(ŷ) → ∞. Cross-entropy punishes confident, wrong predictions brutally — far more than a "meh, 50/50" guess. This is exactly why it trains classifiers to be well-calibrated instead of recklessly confident.

5. KL divergence: the gap between two beliefs

Cross-entropy mixes two things together: the irreducible surprise of P itself, plus the extra cost from your guess Q being wrong. KL divergence isolates just the extra part: 

KL(P ‖ Q) = H(P, Q) − H(P) = Σ p(x) · log( p(x) / q(x) )
H(P,Q) = H(P) + KL(P‖Q). Since H(P) is a constant (it doesn't depend on your model at all), minimizing cross-entropy and minimizing KL divergence are the exact same optimization problem. This is why "train with cross-entropy loss" and "minimize KL to the true distribution" are the same sentence in disguise.
  • KL(P‖Q) ≥ 0 always, with equality only when P = Q exactly (Gibbs' inequality).
  • KL(P‖Q) ≠ KL(Q‖P) in general — it's a divergence, not a distance. No triangle inequality, no symmetry.

6. Forward vs. reverse KL: mode-covering vs. mode-seeking

Because KL is asymmetric, KL(P‖Q) and KL(Q‖P) produce genuinely different fitted models when Q is too simple to represent P exactly (e.g. fitting a single Gaussian to a bimodal target). This distinction quietly explains a lot of ML:

Forward KL: KL(P‖Q)Reverse KL: KL(Q‖P)
Used byMaximum likelihood estimation (standard supervised training)Variational inference (VAEs' ELBO, some RL objectives)
Penalizes mostQ being near-zero where P has mass ("zero-avoiding")Q having mass where P is near-zero ("zero-forcing")
Resulting fitMode-covering: Q spreads out to cover all of P's mass, even if that means putting mass in low-density valleysMode-seeking: Q collapses onto a single mode of P and ignores the rest
You're summarizing a two-humped opinion poll (half the town loves the plan, half hates it) with one average number. Forward KL is a diplomat: it reports "50% support," a number that covers the whole spread but describes no actual voter well. Reverse KL is a pragmatist: it picks the loudest camp and reports their opinion fully, ignoring that the other half exists.

7. The rest of the divergence zoo

KL isn't the only way to compare distributions. Two more come up constantly:

DivergenceIdeaSymmetric?Where you'll see it
Jensen–Shannon (JS) Average of KL(P‖M) and KL(Q‖M), where M = ½(P+Q) Yes, and bounded in [0, log 2] The original GAN's theoretical objective (the discriminator implicitly estimates JS divergence)
Wasserstein / Earth-Mover's Minimum "cost" (mass × distance) to reshape one distribution's pile of dirt into the other's Yes WGAN — stays meaningful (non-zero gradient) even when P and Q have no overlapping support, unlike KL/JS which blow up or flatline
KL and JS both go haywire when P and Q don't overlap: KL → ∞, and JS saturates at log 2 with a near-zero gradient — useless for training. This is precisely the failure mode that motivated Wasserstein GANs: Earth-mover's distance still gives a useful, smooth gradient even for two distributions living on completely disjoint supports.

8. Margin-based & focal losses

Cross-entropy isn't the only way to train a classifier. Hinge loss (the SVM loss) doesn't care about calibrated probabilities at all — it only cares whether you're on the right side of a margin. Let m = y · f(x) where y ∈ {−1,+1} and f(x) is the raw model score: 

Hinge(m)   = max(0, 1 − m)
Logistic(m)= log(1 + e^(−m))     ← this is binary cross-entropy, rewritten in margin form
Hinge gives zero loss (and zero gradient) once you're correctly classified with margin ≥ 1 — it stops caring entirely. Logistic/cross-entropy never fully stops caring; it keeps nudging ŷ toward 1 forever, just with a shrinking gradient. That's why SVMs produce sparse "support vectors" (only the borderline points have nonzero gradient) while logistic regression uses every point a little.

Try it: hinge vs. logistic loss vs. 0-1 loss

0-1 loss (what we actually want, but can't optimize — no gradient) Hinge = max(0, 1−m) Logistic/CE = log(1+e⁻ᵐ)

Focal loss tweaks cross-entropy for a different problem: extreme class imbalance (e.g. object detection, where 99.9% of candidate boxes are "background"). It adds a (1−p_t)^γ down-weighting factor that dampens the loss for examples the model already gets easily right, so training focuses on the hard, misclassified ones. 

FL(p_t) = − (1 − p_t)^γ · log(p_t)        (γ = 0 recovers plain cross-entropy)

Try it: focal loss down-weighting

Increase γ and watch easy examples (p_t near 1) get squashed toward zero loss, while hard examples (p_t near 0) barely change.

Cross-entropy = −log(p_t) Focal = −(1−p_t)^γ·log(p_t)

9. Contrastive, triplet, and InfoNCE losses

These losses don't score a single prediction against a label — they score relationships between examples in embedding space: "these two should be close, that one should be far."

LossFormula (sketch)Idea
Contrastivey·d² + (1−y)·max(0, margin − d)²Pull same-class pairs together (d = embedding distance), push different-class pairs apart until they clear a margin
Tripletmax(0, d(a,p) − d(a,n) + margin)Given anchor/positive/negative, the anchor should be closer to the positive than to the negative by at least margin
InfoNCE−log[ exp(sim(a,p)/τ) / Σ exp(sim(a,i)/τ) ]Softmax cross-entropy over similarity scores — "pick the true positive out of a batch of distractors." Powers CLIP, SimCLR, contrastive pretraining.
Look closely at InfoNCE: it's −log(softmax(...)) — literally categorical cross-entropy, where the "classes" are "which item in the batch is the true match." Contrastive learning didn't invent a new loss family; it repurposed cross-entropy with similarity scores instead of raw logits. The whole page keeps circling back to the same handful of ideas.

10. GANs and VAEs: losses built out of the losses above

Generative model losses look intimidating but they're assembled from exactly the pieces you've already seen.

GAN loss (minimax)

min_G max_D   E[log D(x)]  +  E[log(1 − D(G(z)))]

This is binary cross-entropy, twice: the discriminator D is trained with BCE to tell real from fake; the generator G is trained to make D fail that same BCE. At the optimal discriminator, this objective is provably minimizing the Jensen–Shannon divergence between the real and generated data distributions (§7) — which is also why vanilla GANs suffer from the same vanishing-gradient problem JS divergence has, motivating WGAN's switch to Wasserstein distance.

VAE loss (the ELBO)

L = Reconstruction loss (cross-entropy or MSE, decoder vs. input)
  + KL( q(z|x) ‖ p(z) )       ← reverse-KL regularizer pulling the encoded distribution toward a prior
The VAE loss is literally "§2/§4 + §5/§6" stapled together: a reconstruction term that's just MSE or cross-entropy on pixels, plus a reverse-KL term (§6) that regularizes the encoder's output distribution q(z|x) toward a simple prior p(z) (usually a standard Gaussian). Because it's reverse KL, the encoder tends to be mode-seeking/conservative about spreading probability mass — one of the reasons VAE samples often look a bit "blurry" and averaged-out compared to GAN samples.
