1 The Big Picture

Imagine you're playing a game of Wheel of Fortune. The phrase has 20 blanks. Every "round" of the game, you guess several letters at a time, fill them in, and the remaining blanks stay. This is roughly how a Diffusion Language Model (DLM) works: it iteratively unmasks tokens until the sentence is complete.

The problem? Each round, the model has to re-process the entire sequence from scratch. For a sentence of length $L = 1024$, you need $L$ such rounds, each with full bidirectional attention — yielding a cubic $\mathcal{O}(L^3)$ cost. Painful.

The pitch of dKV-Cache: Even though diffusion LLMs use bidirectional attention (which seems to forbid caching), the K and V states of decoded tokens become stable after the step where they were decoded. So you can cache them — you just have to delay the caching by one step, and only cache decoded tokens (not still-masked ones).

Two complementary recipes come out of this insight:

🎯 dKV-Cache-Decode

Near-lossless. Periodically refreshes the cache. Sometimes even improves quality on long sequences (suggesting baseline DLMs under-use context).

⚡ dKV-Cache-Greedy

Aggressive. Drops complexity from $\mathcal{O}(L^3)$ to $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$ — matching autoregressive models. Trades a small accuracy hit for big speedups.

⏱️ 2× → 10× faster · training-free

2 KV-Cache 101 — How It Works in GPT-Style Models

Before we talk about diffusion, let's nail down what KV-Cache is. If you already know, skim this; if not, this section is essential.

Transformer attention in 30 seconds

Inside every transformer layer, each token computes three projections:

$$\mathbf{Q} = \mathbf{H} \mathbf{W}_Q, \quad \mathbf{K} = \mathbf{H} \mathbf{W}_K, \quad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{H} \mathbf{W}_V$$

where $\mathbf{H}$ is the hidden state. Attention is computed as:

$$\text{Attention}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V}$$

The autoregressive shortcut

In a GPT-style model, you generate one token at a time. The trick: each new token only attends to previous tokens (causal mask). So once you've computed $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ for tokens $1, 2, \ldots, t-1$, those values never change as you generate token $t, t+1, \ldots$ — you can save them in a cache and skip recomputation.

$$\mathbf{z}_t = \text{softmax}\!\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q}_t^{[t]} (\mathbf{K}_t^{[1:t]})^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \mathbf{V}^{[1:t]} \quad \text{with} \quad \begin{cases}\mathbf{K}_t^{[1:t]} = \text{concat}(\mathbf{K}_{t-1}^{[1:t-1]},\, \mathbf{K}_t^{[t]}) \\ \mathbf{V}_t^{[1:t]} = \text{concat}(\mathbf{V}_{t-1}^{[1:t-1]},\, \mathbf{V}_t^{[t]})\end{cases}$$

That's Equation 1 in the paper. Two takeaways:

  1. You only compute K, V for the current token (the $[t]$ index).
  2. You reuse all previous K, V from the cache. Per-step cost: $\mathcal{O}(L)$. Total: $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$.

Why this works: The causal attention mask guarantees that the K, V for token 3 only depend on tokens 1, 2, 3 — not on tokens 4, 5, 6. So once computed, they're frozen forever.

3 Why Standard KV-Cache Breaks in Diffusion LLMs

Diffusion LLMs are wonderful but they break the two foundational assumptions of standard KV-Cache:

Problem 1: Timestep-variant K, V states

In a DLM, attention is bidirectional — every token can attend to every other token. So when token 5 changes from [MASK] to "house" at step $m$, that change propagates: it shifts what the K, V of token 3 would compute. Formally:

$$\mathbf{K}_m^{[1:t-1]} \neq \mathbf{K}_n^{[1:t-1]} \quad \text{if } n \neq m$$

In English: the K of a token changes across diffusion steps. The "freeze and reuse forever" assumption is gone.

Problem 2: Non-sequential decoding order

AR models generate left-to-right. You know in advance that step $t$ produces token $t$. So you know exactly which position needs new Q, K, V.

DLMs generate in any order — picking whichever masked position has the highest confidence, or doing random remasking. So you don't know which token will be decoded next, which makes the targeted "compute only $\mathbf{Q}^{[t]}, \mathbf{K}^{[t]}, \mathbf{V}^{[t]}$" trick impossible.

🟢 AR Model

  • Causal attention → K, V stay fixed
  • Left-to-right → next position known
  • Cost per step: $\mathcal{O}(L)$
  • Total cost: $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$

🔴 Diffusion LM

  • Bidirectional → K, V drift each step
  • Arbitrary order → unknown next position
  • Cost per step: $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$
  • Total cost: $\mathcal{O}(L^3)$ 😬
The math: For $L = 1024$, $L^3$ is ~30,000× more operations than $L^2$. That's why DLMs, despite being conceptually similar in model size, are dramatically slower in practice.

4 The Key Empirical Observation

This is the paper's pivot point. The authors run a careful empirical study of how K, V vectors evolve through the denoising process. They look at:

  1. Pairwise similarity of K, V states across timesteps (cosine similarity heatmap).
  2. Euclidean distance of K, V before vs. after a token is decoded.
  3. For each token, at which steps do K, V change the most?

What they found (three observations)

Observation 1. Despite step-to-step differences, K and V exhibit consistently high similarity across timesteps. Most steps barely change them.
Observation 2. Once a token is decoded, its K and V representation becomes relatively stable in later steps. Still-masked tokens, in contrast, keep fluctuating.
Observation 3. The biggest change in K, V for any given token happens at the step it is decoded. After that step, things settle down.

Simulated visualization (matching Figure 2 in the paper)

For each token, the change is large at the moment it's decoded (diagonal line), then small thereafter. Once decoded, K, V are reusable — that's what dKV-Cache exploits.
The takeaway: Don't try to cache all tokens — that's hopeless. Cache only the decoded tokens, and cache them after they've been decoded (when they're stable).

5 Delayed KV-Cache — The Core Idea

Here's the recipe in two sentences:

(1) Only cache decoded tokens. Masked tokens keep changing — recompute their K, V each step.
(2) Delay caching by one step. When a token just got decoded at step $t$, its K, V are still settling. Wait until step $t+1$ to cache its stable values.

Generalized KV-Cache formulation

The standard AR formula in Eq. 1 used a contiguous slice $[1\!:\!t]$. dKV-Cache replaces that with an arbitrary index set $\mathcal{S}_t \subseteq \mathcal{I} = \{1, \ldots, L\}$ — the set of positions whose K, V we have cached from previous steps:

$$\mathbf{z}_t = \text{softmax}\!\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q}_t^{D_t}\,(\mathbf{K}_t^{\mathcal{S}_t \cup \{D_t\}})^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \mathbf{V}_t^{\mathcal{S}_t \cup \{D_t\}}$$

with $\mathbf{K}_t^{\mathcal{S}_t \cup \{t\}} = \text{concat\_reorder}\!\left(\mathbf{K}_{t-1}^{\mathcal{S}_t}, \mathbf{K}_t^{D_t}\right)$ (and similarly for $\mathbf{V}$)

where $D_t$ is the position being decoded at step $t$. Equation 2.

Key notation:
  • $\mathcal{I}$ = all positions in the answer, $\{1, 2, \ldots, L\}$.
  • $\mathcal{M}_t$ = the still-masked set at step $t$ (not yet finalized).
  • $D_t$ = the token being decoded at step $t$.
  • $\mathcal{S}_t = \mathcal{I} \setminus \mathcal{M}_t$ = positions already decoded (so cacheable).
  • $\text{concat\_reorder}$ = an operator that interleaves the cached K, V with the new ones in correct positional order.

6 One-Step Delayed Caching — Why It Matters

The crucial subtlety. When a token is decoded at step $t$, its K and V have just undergone the biggest change of their lives (Observation 3). They're not stable yet. If we cache $\mathbf{K}_t^{D_t}$ at step $t$ and reuse it at step $t+1$, we get errors.

So we delay: at step $t$, we use the masking state from step $t-1$ to decide what's cacheable. This is Equation 4 in the paper, the definition of dKV-Cache-Decode:

$$\mathbf{z}_t = \text{softmax}\!\left( \frac{\mathbf{Q}_t^{\mathcal{M}_{t-1}}\,(\mathbf{K}_t^{\mathcal{I}})^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \mathbf{V}_t^{\mathcal{I}}$$

with $\mathbf{K}_t^{\mathcal{I}} = \text{concat\_reorder}\!\left(\mathbf{K}_{t-1}^{\mathcal{I} \setminus \mathcal{M}_{t-1}},\, \mathbf{K}_t^{\mathcal{M}_{t-1}}\right)$ (and similarly $\mathbf{V}$)

In plain English: at step $t$, we recompute K, V for all tokens that were masked at step $t-1$ (including the one we just decoded), and we cache K, V from step $t-1$ for everything else.

Why this looks weird but is right

  • Token $D_{t-1}$ was decoded at step $t-1$. Its K, V at that moment were unstable.
  • At step $t$, we recompute its K, V — now it sees the full updated context, so it's stable.
  • From step $t+1$ onward, $D_{t-1}$ is in $\mathcal{S}_t$ and gets cached.
What happens without the delay? The paper shows in Figure 3: without one-step delay, accuracy collapses to near-zero as the cache ratio increases. With the delay, accuracy stays nearly lossless even at very high cache reuse.

Cache refreshing

To handle minor representational drift over long sequences, dKV-Cache occasionally refreshes the cache — discarding it and recomputing K, V from scratch. The paper uses refresh intervals of 4 or 8 steps for Dream, 8 for LLaDA. The cost is small but helps maintain consistency.

# Pseudocode: dKV-Cache-Decode core loop
for t in range(1, T+1):
    if t % refresh_interval == 0:
        cache = {}                       # refresh: empty the cache
        forward_pass(full_sequence)
    else:
        M_prev = masked_set_at(t-1)
        # Recompute K, V for positions in M_prev
        K_new, V_new = compute_KV(positions=M_prev)
        # Reuse cached K, V for the rest
        K_full = concat_reorder(cache.K, K_new)
        V_full = concat_reorder(cache.V, V_new)
        # Run attention
        out = attention(Q[M_prev], K_full, V_full)
        # Update cache for tokens that are no longer masked
        cache.update(positions=M_prev - M_curr)

7 Variant 1: dKV-Cache-Decode (Lossless)

This is the version we've been discussing. Properties:

✓ Near-lossless

Often indistinguishable from baseline, sometimes better on long sequences.

✓ Long-context boost

+2 to +7 accuracy points on tasks with long outputs (suggests baselines under-use context).

⚠ Still $\mathcal{O}(L^3)$

Complexity unchanged asymptotically. But constant is much smaller (~2–3× practical speedup).

Why is it still $\mathcal{O}(L^3)$?

At each step we recompute K, V for the set $\mathcal{M}_{t-1}$ — the still-masked tokens. At the start of denoising, $|\mathcal{M}_t| \approx L$, so each step is $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$ work. Over $L$ steps, that's $\mathcal{O}(L^3)$.

However, the per-step cost shrinks as more tokens get decoded — so by the end, we're only recomputing a few positions. The aggregate speedup is real but not asymptotic.

🧠 Why might dKV-Cache-Decode improve accuracy on long sequences (e.g., GSM8K accuracy 80.97% → 83.13%)?
A. The cache reduces noise from random masking.
B. Caching forces the model to keep stable, contextually-grounded representations from earlier steps, mitigating some over-correction in late steps.
C. The model gets to see more parameters because of the cache.

8 Variant 2: dKV-Cache-Greedy (faster, $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$)

The lossless variant still needs $\mathcal{O}(L^3)$ work because $|\mathcal{M}_t|$ is large early on. To match the AR complexity, dKV-Cache-Greedy is more aggressive about what it recomputes.

The recipe

At each step $t$, we restrict $\mathcal{M}_t$ to just three components:

  1. $D_t$ — the token currently being decoded.
  2. $D_{t-1}$ — the previous decoded token (motivated by one-step delayed caching: it needs its K, V refreshed once).
  3. Local window $\mathcal{W}(t)$ — neighboring tokens around $D_t$, with a small fixed window size (the paper uses $w = 6$).

Mathematically:

$$\mathcal{M}_t = \{D_t\} \cup \{D_{t-1}\} \cup \mathcal{W}(t), \quad \mathcal{W}(t) = \left\{ x_i \mid i \in \left[D_t - \lfloor w/2 \rfloor,\, D_t + \lfloor w/2 \rfloor\right] \right\}$$

With $|\mathcal{M}_t| \leq w + 2 \approx 8$ (a constant!), the per-step cost is $\mathcal{O}(L)$ and the total cost is $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$. Same complexity as a standard AR model.

The trade-off

Why "greedy"? By caching even still-masked tokens (outside the window) and not refreshing them, we accept that their K, V are stale. For tokens far from $D_t$, this is empirically fine. For nearby tokens (within $w$), we still recompute — which is why the window matters. The result: small accuracy hit, big speed win.

The paper shows dKV-Cache-Greedy outperforms few-step baselines (where you simply use fewer denoising steps) on most benchmarks at similar throughput.

9 Variants 3 & 4: dKV-Cache-Prefill & PD

Long prompts (think: 8-shot in-context learning) dominate runtime cost. The authors carve out two more variants for this setting.

📌 dKV-Cache-Prefill

Cache the K, V of the prompt tokens once, then never refresh them. Experiments show prompt tokens "primarily attend to each other" — they're insulated from changes in the decoded answer.

Key result: up to 18.91× speedup on GPQA (long prompt, short generation).

🔄 dKV-Cache-PD

Mixed strategy: cache prefill tokens without refresh (like Prefill), but intermittently refresh the cache of newly decoded tokens. Best of both worlds.

Inspired by the disaggregation of prefill/decoding phases in LLM serving systems.

When to use which variant?

ScenarioRecommended variantWhy
Short prompt, long output, accuracy mattersdKV-Cache-DecodeLossless, may even improve quality.
Short prompt, long output, speed mattersdKV-Cache-Greedy$\mathcal{O}(L^2)$ complexity.
Long prompt, short output (few-shot ICL)dKV-Cache-PrefillPrompt dominates runtime — cache it once.

11 Summary & Comparison with Fast-dLLM

One-sentence summary: dKV-Cache is a training-free KV-cache for diffusion language models that exploits the empirical observation that K, V vectors of decoded tokens stabilize after their decoding step — with the crucial twist of delaying caching by one step to capture the most stable representation.

Five key takeaways

  1. Cache only decoded tokens. Masked tokens keep changing across denoising steps — recompute them.
  2. Delay caching by one step. The K, V at the exact moment of decoding are unstable; wait one step for them to settle.
  3. Two complexity regimes. Decode variant: $\mathcal{O}(L^3)$ with low constant. Greedy variant: $\mathcal{O}(L^2)$ matching AR models.
  4. Long prefill is the biggest win. Caching prompts once delivers up to 18.91× speedup on long-context, short-generation tasks.
  5. Sometimes caching improves quality. On HumanEval and long-output GSM8K, stable representations help the model use context more effectively.

How does this compare to Fast-dLLM?

Both papers tackle the same problem from May 2025, but with different philosophies:

AspectdKV-Cache (this paper)Fast-dLLM
Caching granularityPer-token (token-by-token)Block-wise (fixed-size blocks, e.g. 32)
ApproximationOne-step delay, refresh every NApproximate within block, recompute between blocks
Parallel decodingNot the focus — caching onlyConfidence-aware parallel decoding is a core contribution
Theoretical analysisEmpirical (representation similarity)Theorem 1: equivalence under high confidence
Max speedup reported~19× (long prefill)~27.6× (long generation)
When most usefulLong prefill, ICL, careful accuracyLong generation, throughput-focused
They're complementary. In principle, you could combine dKV-Cache's delayed token-level caching with Fast-dLLM's confidence-aware parallel decoding. The papers tackle orthogonal axes of the speedup problem.

Limitations & open questions

  • The "best" cache refresh interval is dataset-dependent — needs tuning.
  • The greedy variant relies on a predefined decoding order; combining with confidence-based remasking is non-trivial.
  • How does the method interact with KV-cache compression schemes (quantization, eviction)?
  • The "improvement on long sequences" effect is interesting but not theoretically explained.
